\section{Collision Probabilities of Centera CA}\label{sec:5}

Centera has three naming schemes, referred to as \textbf{M}, \textbf{GM}
and \textbf{M++.} The \textbf{M} naming scheme returns a 128-bit CA that
is simply the MD5 hash of the associated content. The \textbf{GM} naming
scheme returns a 256-bit CA that is comprised of an MD5 hash of the
associated content, a random bit-string, timestamp, counter and header
bits. The \textbf{M++} naming scheme returns a 256-bit CA that is
comprised of the MD5 and a truncated SHA-256 hash of the associated
content. Detail for \textbf{M} is provided in \S\ref{sec:5.2};
\textbf{GM} detail is provided in \S\ref{sec:5.4}; \textbf{M++}
is treated in \S\ref{sec:5.5}. For now the salient point is that
the various naming schemes return CAs of different bit size (128 bits
for \textbf{M} and 256 bits for \textbf{GM} and \textbf{M++}), and hence
have differing collision probabilities and preimage resistance.

\subsection{Birthday Paradox}\label{sec:5.1}\label{birthday-paradox}

For uniformly and independently distributed birthdays, 253 \emph{other}
people give approximately a 50\% chance of matching a specified person's
birthday (254 people total if that person is in the room):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.1}
1-\left(\frac{364}{365}\right)^{253}\approx0.50.
\end{equation}

However, if you change the question to include one key difference, the
answer changes dramatically.

The modified question is: how many people need to be present in a room
for \emph{any two} people to have \(\approx50\%\) chance of sharing the
same birthday? Answer: 23. This number is surprisingly low, which leads
to this phenomenon being given appellations such as ``birthday
surprise'' or ``birthday paradox.'' The surprisingly small result for
the second question becomes clear when you consider that with 23 people
in the room there are 253 \emph{pairs} of people in the room; i.e.,
\(23\cdot22/2=253.\) Of course, for this result to hold the people in
the room must have an even (random) distribution of birthdays; clearly
this result would not stand if the room was full of twins.

We now add some rigor and generalization to the birthday paradox.

\textbf{Theorem (birthday upper bound):} Let \(P_{\mathrm{collision}}(N,q)\) denote the
probability to have at least one collision when we throw \emph{q
\textgreater{}} 1 balls at random into \emph{N \textgreater{} q}
buckets, giving us equation \eqref{eq:5.2} below.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.2}
P_{\mathrm{collision}}(N,q)\le0.5\frac{q(q-1)}{N}
\end{equation}

The union bound below is useful for rare collisions. The exact probability
and its exponential approximation are given after the proof, so that a
bound is not mistaken for an equality. Additional birthday bounds and
analysis are discussed in \cite{ref26}.

\textbf{Proof:} Let \(C_i\) be the event that the \(i\) -th ball is
thrown in a non-empty bucket, i.e.~it collides with one of the previous
balls. Then \(P[C_i]\) is at most \((i-1)/N\) since when the \emph{i}-th
ball is thrown, there are at most \(i-1\) different occupied buckets and
the \(i\) -th ball is equally likely to land in any of them. Now we
have,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.3}
\begin{aligned}P_{\mathrm{collision}}(N,q)&=P[C_1\lor C_2\lor\cdots\lor C_q]\\&\le P[C_1]+P[C_2]+\cdots+P[C_q]\\&\le\frac0N+\frac1N+\frac2N+\cdots+\frac{q-1}{N}\\&\le\frac{q(q-1)}{2N}.\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The bound in \eqref{eq:5.2} is useful when collisions are rare, but it is
not the probability itself. For $0\le q\le N$ independent uniform samples,
\[
P_{\mathrm{collision}}(N,q)
 =1-\prod_{i=0}^{q-1}\left(1-\frac{i}{N}\right)
 \approx1-\exp\left(-\frac{q(q-1)}{2N}\right).
\]
The exponential approximation applies near the birthday scale for large
$N$; the linear approximation $q(q-1)/(2N)$ requires that this ratio be small.
At $q\approx\sqrt N$, the probability is approximately $1-e^{-1/2}=39.35\%$.
The 50\% threshold is approximately $\sqrt{2N\ln2}=1.17741\sqrt N$.
These statements assume independence and uniformity, not merely an even
histogram of outputs.

\subsection{Centera with the M naming
scheme}\label{sec:5.2}\label{centera-with-the-m-naming-scheme}

For distinct, non-adversarial contents, assume MD5 outputs behave as
independent uniform 128-bit values. Statistical tests motivate this model
but do not prove it, and it does not apply to chosen collision attacks.
Set $N=2^{128}$. At $q=2^{64}$ the collision probability is approximately
39.35\%; the 50\% threshold is approximately $1.17741\cdot2^{64}$.
For $q=\alpha2^{64}$, the union bound becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.4}
P_{\mathrm{collision}}(2^{128},q)
 \le\min\left\{1,\frac{\alpha2^{64}(\alpha2^{64}-1)}{2\cdot2^{128}}\right\}.
\end{equation}
When $q\gg1$ and $\alpha\ll1$, the bound and the probability are both
approximately $\alpha^2/2$.

significant figures. Capacities are decimal \emph{logical payload} totals,
not physical protected-storage requirements: 1 kB is 1,000 bytes.

At 10,000 files per second for 1,000 years of 365 days each,
$q=3.1536\cdot10^{14}$ and the modeled probability is approximately
$1.46\cdot10^{-10}$. A total of $10^{15}$ files of 1 kB represents 1 EB
of logical payload and takes about 3,171 years at that rate. Protection
and metadata overhead require additional physical capacity.

For adversarial collisions, MD5's cryptanalytic weaknesses replace this
random-input model \cite{ref30,ref31}. Reusing a known collision pair is
not the same task as constructing one for application-constrained content.

For a \emph{given} file, finding a distinct file with the same M address
is a second-preimage problem. Reference~\cite{ref11} gives a generic
long-message attack with estimated work
$k2^{65}+2^{129-k}$ MD5 compression calls for a target near $2^k$ blocks.
The historical ``100Mb'' limit is ambiguous and is not independently
verified. Interpreting it as 100 MB ($10^8$ bytes) or 100 MiB
($100\cdot2^{20}$ bytes) gives fewer than $2^{21}$ padded 512-bit blocks;
100 megabits is smaller still. Substituting $k=21$ gives roughly
$2^{108}$ calls as a long-message reference scale, not a proved minimum
for all files or attacks. Shorter targets have different work estimates.

\subsection{Random Generators}\label{sec:5.3}\label{random-generators}

In order to move towards the stronger GM naming scheme, we need the
following definitions from the HAC \cite{ref2}.

\emph{Definition 6}: A \textbf{pseudorandom bit generator} (PRBG) is a
deterministic algorithm which, given a truly random binary sequence of
length \emph{k}, outputs a binary sequence of length \(l\ge k\) which
``appears'' to be random. The input to the PRBG is called the
\emph{seed}, while the output of the PRBG is called a \emph{pseudorandom
bit sequence}.

\emph{Definition 7}: A pseudorandom bit generator is said to pass the
\textbf{next-bit test} if there is no polynomial-time algorithm which,
on input of the first \emph{l} bits of an output sequence \emph{s}, can
predict the \emph{(l+1)}\textsuperscript{\emph{st}} bit of \emph{s} with
probability significantly greater than \(\frac12\).

\emph{Definition 8}: A PRBG that passes the next-bit test is called a
cryptographically secure pseudorandom bit generator (CSPRBG).

\subsection{Centera with the GM naming
scheme}\label{sec:5.4}\label{centera-with-the-gm-naming-scheme}

Although the probabilities of having collisions with the M naming scheme
are minimal, a second naming scheme, called GM, was introduced which
includes additional random bits in the resultant CA. This additional
randomness has the effect of further reducing the probability of
collisions.

The Centera GM CA contains the following components:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{M:} a 128 bit MD5 hash based on the file content;
\item
  \textbf{G:} a 70 bit random string generated by a cryptographically
  secure pseudorandom bit generator;
\item
  \textbf{T:} a 35 bit timestamp, with second resolution (actually, one
  unit of \textbf{T} is 1024 milliseconds);
\item
  \textbf{C:} a 10 bit counter; and
\item
  \textbf{H:} 13 bits used as ``header'' codes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Timestamp and counter}\label{sec:5.4.1}

Use $\Delta=1{,}024$ milliseconds per timestamp tick and $C_0=2^{10}=1{,}024$
counter values. A 35-bit timestamp wraps after $2^{35}\Delta$ milliseconds,
about 1,116 years of 365 days. The following bound assumes coherent,
monotone timestamp bins with no wrap, rollback, or reuse during the interval;
otherwise objects with repeated timestamp values must be grouped together.

Let $A$ be the number of access nodes and $m$ an integer upper bound on
writes assigned to any one timestamp by any one node. If $S$ is expressed
in files per second, using $m=\lceil1.024S\rceil$ requires a bound on writes
\emph{within each tick}; a long-term average rate alone is insufficient.
Each node's counter advances by one per write and does not reset within
a tick. A given counter value can then occur at most $\lceil m/C_0\rceil$
times per node. Consequently, for any fixed $(T,C)$ bin,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.5}
n_{T,C}\le B,\qquad B=A\left\lceil\frac{m}{C_0}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
This bound keeps the integer quantization and holds without requiring
$S>A$. It does not depend on the nodes having independent counter offsets.

\subsubsection{Applying the Birthday Paradox}\label{sec:5.4.2}

Two distinct contents can share a full GM address only if M, G, T, C, and H
all match. Ignoring H gives a conservative bound. For the accidental-content
model, assume that each candidate pair in a $(T,C)$ bin has probability
$2^{-198}$ of matching both its 128-bit M and 70-bit G components. This
requires independent uniform M outputs and fresh uniform G values independent
of the content, node, counter, and timestamp. A CSPRBG definition alone does
not prove these properties of an implementation. A union bound gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.6}
P_{T,C}\le\frac{\binom{n_{T,C}}2}{2^{198}}
          \le\frac{B(B-1)}{2^{199}}.
\end{equation}
No independence between collision events is needed for this union bound.
For $Z$ elapsed milliseconds, at most
$L=\lceil Z/\Delta\rceil+1$ timestamp bins intersect the interval, allowing
for partial ticks at both ends. There are at most $LC_0$ candidate bins:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.7}
P_{\mathrm{GM}}\le
 \min\left\{1,\frac{LC_0B(B-1)}{2^{199}}\right\}.
\end{equation}
For repeated identical content, or adversarially arranged MD5 collisions,
M contributes no independent collision protection. With fresh independent
uniform G values, replace $2^{198}$ in the pair model by $2^{70}$;
\eqref{eq:5.7} then has denominator $2^{71}$. The stronger 198-bit model
must not be applied to those cases.

\subsubsection{Collision Probability with GM}\label{sec:5.4.3}

For the historical illustrative workload $A=100$, $m=10{,}240$ writes per
tick per node (10,000 files/second under the per-tick assumption), and
1,000 years of 365 days, $Z=31{,}536{,}000{,}000{,}000$ milliseconds.
Thus $B=1{,}000$ and $L=30{,}796{,}875{,}001$, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:5.8}
P_{\mathrm{GM}}\le
\frac{30{,}796{,}875{,}001\cdot1{,}024\cdot1{,}000\cdot999}{2^{199}}
\approx3.92\cdot10^{-41}.
\end{equation}
This is a conditional upper bound, not impossibility and not an empirical
measurement of Centera. It must not be compared directly with a per-bit disk
error rate without specifying how many bits are read and over what interval.
A content checksum can help detect corruption; a successful match is not an
absolute guarantee of integrity.

For an attacker, fresh independent server-chosen G values give at most
$\binom u2/2^{70}$ collision probability among $u$ compatible writes in one
$(T,C,H)$ group whose M values have already been made equal. Matching a
\emph{fixed} target G in $u$ independent compatible trials has probability
$1-(1-2^{-70})^u$. These are trial-count statements; converting them into
elapsed years requires evidence about accepted write rates and timestamp
control.

The archived 2005 correspondence reports that a raw-write SDK path can allow
caller control of G. Therefore server-chosen randomness and exclusively
online attacks cannot be asserted for every write path. If G is selectable,
its random-collision factor provides no security against that caller;
control of the other fields and the system's write checks must be analyzed.
An address collision alone does not establish overwrite capability.

\subsection{Centera with the M++ naming
scheme}\label{sec:5.5}\label{centera-with-the-m-naming-scheme-1}

The historical M++ construction concatenates 128 MD5 bits, eight formatting
bits, and 120 SHA-256 output bits. Its deterministic addressing supports
identical-content naming. The original paper describes GM deduplication as
a planned release feature; that release history is not established here.

For accidental collisions on distinct contents, assume the joint MD5 and
truncated SHA-256 output is uniform over $N=2^{248}$ values and independent
between contents. This is a modeling assumption about the joint output,
not a consequence of adding bit lengths. At $q=2^{124}$ the birthday
probability is approximately 39.35\%, and the 50\% threshold is approximately
$1.17741\cdot2^{124}$. At 10,000 files per second for 1,000 years of 365 days,
$q=3.1536\cdot10^{14}$ gives approximately $1.10\cdot10^{-46}$, compared
with $1.46\cdot10^{-10}$ for M under its random-input model.

For adversarial inputs, Joux's multicollision construction shows that
concatenated iterated hashes need not achieve the collision strength of an
ideal hash with their combined output length \cite{ref1}. This does not
justify a general claim that every truncated concatenation has exactly the
strength of its strongest component.

The original illustrative attack builds a $2^{60}$-member MD5 multicollision
family and searches for a collision in 120 SHA-256 output bits. It presumes
that suitable equal-length MD5 collision blocks can be chained and that the
resulting SHA-256 prefixes behave randomly. A naive scan of $2^{60}$
candidates of 120 message blocks costs about $120\cdot2^{60}$ SHA-256
compression calls, plus padding, MD5-family construction, and storage/search
costs. This is a conditional attack estimate, not a lower bound on required
work or a proof that future improvements cannot help. Its birthday success
probability at that family size is about 39\%, not certainty.

For second preimages, the original $2^{119}$ claim is not established by
\cite{ref11}. SHA-256 has a 256-bit chaining state even when only 120 output
bits are retained; substituting 120 for its state width in that attack is
invalid. Moreover, a second preimage of M++ must match both the MD5 and
SHA-256 portions. A defensible concrete attack cost for that combined target
is not derived here. The distinction between a fixed target and any of many
targets in \S\ref{sec:4.2} also applies.

\subsection{Summary of naming schemes}\label{sec:5.6}\label{summary-of-naming-schemes}

The introduction of the \textbf{GM} and \textbf{M++} naming scheme
illustrates the flexibility of the Centera architecture, specifically
designed to allow for seamlessly switching amongst naming schemes.
Cryptography will continue to evolve, and due to the high importance in
the commercial and military world, hash functions will be under
continuous scrutiny. Therefore, it is important that Centera is able to
adapt its content addressing schemes to reflect the state of the art in
cryptology.
